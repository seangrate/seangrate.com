Is this term unusual?

Slides 8–11 · KL divergence and the chance band

KL divergence D(p‖q) measures, in bits, how far a term’s grades are from history. The grey band is how far a class of that size drifts by chance alone, 95% of the time. The past terms show how much this course really varies from term to term.

Distance from history, term by term

Each past term is compared with the pooled history of the other past terms; this term with all of them. A past term outside its band means real differences between terms (exams, cohorts), not only chance.

The KL dial

Slides 14–16 · weighing history against this term

The target pα ∝ q1−α · sα, with s this term’s no-curve grades, is the distribution that minimizes (1−α)·D(p‖q) + α·D(p‖s). α is the bet from slide 12: the weight on “the cohort really differs.” Cutoffs then come from ranks, and tied scores get the higher grade.

5

When the truth is known: a simulation

Slides 12 and 14 · which bet pays off

Real scores cannot say whether a low term means a hard exam or a weaker cohort. A simulation can. Each simulated term draws a cohort shift (the students really are stronger or weaker) and an exam shift (the exam was harder or easier than usual). A student’s earned grade is the no-curve grade they would have received on a typical exam. Each rule is scored by how many students it misgrades.

How the numbers are computed
H(p) = −Σg pg log₂ pg     D(p‖q) = Σg pg log₂(pg / qg)

History. The grades of every past term ticked “In history,” pooled by student. Terms entered as scores are graded on the no-curve scale; terms entered as counts are used as given. If some grade never occurs, half a student is added to each grade so that no share is exactly 0.

Chance band. If a class of N were a random draw from history, G = 2N·ln 2·D is about χ² with k − 1 degrees of freedom (Sanov’s theorem gives the exponent). The band is the 95th percentile: about 6.8/N bits for five grades and 14.2/N for twelve. Students in one class are not independent draws, so treat the band as a floor. With twelve grades some expected counts are small, so the band is rougher.

Plus/minus. Standard cutoffs: A 93, A− 90, B+ 87, B 83, B− 80, C+ 77, C 73, C− 70, D+ 67, D 63, D− 60; grade points 4.0, 3.7, 3.3, 3.0, 2.7, 2.3, 2.0, 1.7, 1.3, 1.0, 0.7, 0. “Below C” counts C− and lower; “share of A’s” counts A and A−. Merging grades can only lower D(p‖q), so a shift inside a letter (B+ to B−) is visible only on the plus/minus scale. A+ is counted as A. The bell curve splits each letter’s one-standard-deviation band into thirds.

Rules. No curve: the cutoffs from step 1. Top score raised to 100: everyone gets the gap between the best score and 100. Median at least T: if the median is below T, everyone gets the difference. Bell curve: grades by z-score bands one standard deviation wide around the mean. Curve to history: cutoffs by rank so the class matches history. KL dial: the target above, then cutoffs by rank; with the guard, each cutoff is the lower of the KL cutoff and the no-curve cutoff.

Replaying rules on past terms. Each past term is curved against the pooled history of the other past terms, so no term is compared with itself.

Simulation. 400 simulated terms of this term’s size. Students are drawn from your past terms with scores, each term re-centered on the overall mean; with no past scores, from this term. Each term adds a cohort shift c ~ N(0, σc) to every student and subtracts an exam shift e ~ N(0, σe) from every score. Earned grade: no-curve grade of the score plus c. Each rule sees only the observed scores. The same random draws are reused as you move the sliders, so the curves change smoothly. When both shifts vary, the best α is usually near σc² / (σc² + σe²), the share of term-to-term variation that comes from the cohort.

The example department is synthetic. Fall 2022 and Spring 2023 are entered as grade counts only, the way a registrar report would give them.
